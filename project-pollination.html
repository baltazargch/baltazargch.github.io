Pollination under climate extremes

Bees and the flowers they depend on don't become vulnerable in the same places, or in the same months. Conservation planned around the bees alone misses half the problem — and the mismatch is largest exactly where California's agriculture is most exposed.

The problem

Pollinator conservation is usually planned as if bees were the whole system: identify where bee populations are at risk, protect or restore habitat there. But a bee needs flowers blooming at the moment it is foraging. If warming pushes bloom earlier while bee emergence holds, or shifts the two into different parts of the landscape, a site can look secure for the bees and still fail as a pollination system.

Nobody had mapped that mismatch at the scale a state agency would need to act on it, and heat waves — not gradual warming — are what California's growers and pollinators actually face.

Approach

I modelled vulnerability separately for bees and for their floral resources, then compared the two surfaces in space and across the season rather than collapsing them into a single risk score. The work sits inside an automated, reproducible pipeline, so projections can be re-run when new climate or occurrence data land instead of being rebuilt by hand.

  • Species distribution models for wild bees and their floral hosts, projected under climate scenarios
  • Phenological overlap assessed month by month, not annually averaged
  • Heat-wave frequency and intensity projections layered over agricultural land use
  • Focused application to the blue orchard bee, a managed native pollinator for California orchards

What came of it

The results change the targeting question from where are bees at risk to where and when does the bee–flower system come apart. Those are different maps, and the second one is the actionable one.

The findings are in revision at Communications Biology, and the applied version is going into California's Fifth Climate Change Assessment as a technical report for the California Natural Resources Agency — which is the route by which this reaches state planning rather than only the literature. A parallel strand of the project, led by Argueta-Guzmán, examines how projected heat waves disrupt pollination services in agriculture directly.
